Squarespace and Wix: where website builders hold your traffic back

November 21, 2026 · 6 min read · Sync Race Studios

Squarespace and Wix make it easy to get a site online in an afternoon. That convenience comes with limits, and most of them show up later, in how well the site is found.

First, what builders do well

It's worth being fair. Hosted builders take care of servers, security updates and SSL for you, and their templates are tested on phones. Speed in particular is not the problem many people assume it is: public Core Web Vitals data from the HTTP Archive regularly shows Wix and Squarespace sites passing Google's speed checks more often than the average WordPress site.

So if a builder site is losing you traffic, it's usually not because it loads slowly. It's because of what you can't control.

You only get the controls the platform gives you

Search engines and AI assistants read the code behind a page, not just what it looks like. On a builder you can edit what the platform exposes, page titles, descriptions and a few settings, and not much underneath. That affects things like:

  • Schema markup. The structured data that tells Google and AI search exactly what your business is, where it operates, your services, prices and FAQs. Builders add some automatically, but adding exactly what your business needs is often limited or awkward.
  • Page structure. Headings, the order content loads in, and the extra scripts every page carries are set by the template, not by what's best for your content.
  • URLs and site structure. Builders impose their own URL patterns for blogs, products and collections, which aren't always the clean, descriptive addresses you'd choose.
  • Files for AI crawlers. Things like llms.txt or a carefully tuned robots.txt are either unavailable or limited.

Templates make you look like everyone else

Thousands of businesses start from the same handful of templates. Customers notice, and so does search: when your pages share the same structure and stock phrasing as everyone else in your industry, there is less to set you apart. Original, specific content about your business, your area and your services is what earns rankings and AI citations.

You rent it, you don’t own it

A builder site lives inside the builder. Stop paying and it goes offline, and you can't simply pick up the site and move it elsewhere; moving usually means rebuilding. Monthly plan prices also tend to rise as you add features like booking, memberships or extra storage.

What to ask for instead

Whoever builds your site, builder or not, ask these questions:

  • Is every page rendered as finished HTML, so search engines and AI can read all of it?
  • Can you add schema markup for my business, services, prices and FAQs?
  • Will the site be added to Google Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools, with a sitemap submitted?
  • Can I choose my own URLs and page structure?
  • What happens to the site if I stop paying you?
